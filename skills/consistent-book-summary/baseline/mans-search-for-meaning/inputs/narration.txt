Man’s Search for Meaning, by Viktor E. Frankl, begins with a question that is difficult to look away from: what can remain when nearly everything is taken away?

Before the war, Frankl was a Viennese psychiatrist developing a therapy centered on meaning. In 1942, he and his family were deported by Nazi authorities. His manuscript was confiscated. His family was separated, and most of them were murdered. Frankl survived several camps, including Auschwitz and Türkheim. He writes not as a distant historian, but as a doctor studying what captivity did to ordinary people—and what might help them endure.

He describes three broad phases. First came shock: arrival, uncertainty, and the mind’s desperate hope that the worst might somehow be avoided. Then came camp routine, where hunger and danger could numb feeling. Prisoners tried to preserve small ties to life: a memory, a promise, a scrap of work, a moment of beauty. After liberation came another struggle. Freedom did not instantly erase trauma. Some survivors felt detached, bitter, or lost as they returned to a world that had moved on.

Frankl’s central idea is not that suffering is good, or that attitude can make cruelty acceptable. It is that even when circumstances cannot be changed, a person may still choose how to meet them—and must never be blamed for being unable to do so.

In the book’s second part, Frankl calls his approach logotherapy: healing through the search for meaning. He sees meaning in three places: work or responsibility, love and encounter, and the stance we take toward suffering that cannot be avoided. These are not slogans that solve every hardship. They are ways to notice what life is asking of us in a particular moment.

A task unfinished can call someone forward. Love can keep another person vividly present, even across distance. And when pain cannot be escaped, dignity may survive in a choice of response. Frankl’s pages return to these ideas through small, concrete moments—not easy victories, but moments that resist despair.

Man’s Search for Meaning is both a witness to history and an argument about responsibility. It does not promise that every life will feel meaningful, or that meaning removes grief. Instead, Frankl turns the question around: rather than asking only what we expect from life, what answer can we give it today?
